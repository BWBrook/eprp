## 1. One ancient world, several later histories

Barry's recalled rock–diversity studies, and Claude's branch P, suggest a
strong question: **how differently could we reconstruct the same biological
past after different histories of preservation and discovery?**

Imagine two copies of an ancient marine world. Their organisms and habitats
are initially identical. Much later, one copy loses large sections to erosion,
burial or tectonic destruction; the other retains them. Different fragments
then become accessible and attract collectors. Could one account of that
world suggest a prolonged evolutionary rise and the other a relatively stable
diversity? Which features of the reconstructed history would remain recognisable?

This thought experiment separates three histories that we often compress into
one curve: life and deposition; the subsequent fate of the deposits; and the
changing process of finding and interpreting them. It offers several branches.

**Let the witnesses vary while the past stays fixed.** An ideal comparison
would join ancient settings that were similar biologically and environmentally
but acquired very different later histories. The ambition is to learn how
geology changes the account we obtain. In real comparisons, similar settings
are only candidate counterparts, not literal copies of one biological world.

**Observe the same past at different dates of discovery.** Consider successive
historical collections, newly accessible exposures, borehole records or
well-defined additions to a museum catalogue. The ancient world does not
change when a new collection enters our evidence. Could a proposed account of
sampling predict *what kind* of discovery will next change the curve, and
where? Predicting missing ecological roles or geographic components would be
more revealing than predicting just another number of occurrences. Changes
in taxonomy and dating would themselves be separate changes in the account.

**Make a young record inherit an old record's geological fate.** In an imagined
world, remove whole depositional settings, sedimentary packages and regions
according to plausible subsequent histories. Do apparent rises, plateaux or
crises change character? Here the unit of loss is geological, with its
ecological contents, rather than a random handful of fossil rows. This could
eventually become a simulation, but today it is a way to expose what we mean
by a geological explanation.

**Let organisms alter the durability of their witnesses.** Now relax the fixed
archive assumption. Suppose an ecological innovation changes both the living
world and the material from which its future record is built. A lineage could
gain prominence in our reconstruction partly through its effects on the
archive. Conversely, innovations that destroy a former preservation setting
could make ecological replacements appear more complete than they were.
Could histories of ecological dominance and dominance in the surviving record
systematically diverge? This is an elaboration of F3, not a demonstrated
process in any of the proposed cases.

Three distinctions keep this branch productive. A geological event occurring
after organisms lived cannot alter those organisms retrospectively, but its
location and selectivity can still share causes with their original habitats.
It is therefore a **candidate source of observation variation**, not
automatically a valid instrumental variable. Nor does dividing sampled
richness by surviving rock yield biological richness: different settings,
abundances and detection probabilities give different accumulation curves.
Finally, a rich fauna in little surviving rock is a provocative mismatch,
but concentrated preservation, collecting and time averaging can also create
such a pattern. These possibilities give us alternative histories to develop;
they do not dispose of Barry's question.

## 2. A crisis can spend protection without causing an extinction

Claude's N1 asks what protected the biosphere when a perturbation failed to
kill. Add A3's legacies of extinct builders, and the protection itself becomes
something with a history.

Imagine a world whose soils, reef structures, nutrient stores or spatial
refuges buffer a disturbance. Species counts change little. Yet the event
consumes a store, dismantles a structure or erodes the diversity of refuges
that made the apparent resilience possible. A second, smaller disturbance
then causes a major loss. The first event was biologically quiet but left a
large change in vulnerability.

**Could some extinctions be delayed consequences of earlier perturbations that
looked harmless?** A sequence of non-events might be accumulating damage,
replenishing protection or strengthening it. Those are different imagined
worlds, all compatible with low immediate extinction after the first shock.
The sequence and spacing become part of the scientific question.

This also opens a third version of our ageing-versus-maturation argument.
A world might become better protected against familiar disturbances while
becoming less able to reorganise under unfamiliar ones. Dependencies that
improve routine functioning could constrain alternative responses. It would
then mature in one respect and age in another. The simpler worlds—general
robustness increasing, or general flexibility decreasing—remain competing
possibilities; the mixed version does not reconcile them by definition.

An interesting imagined history would apply the same disturbances in a
different order. Which legacy is depleted, rebuilt or newly created? Does the
last crisis depend on its own magnitude, or on what earlier events left
available to absorb it? This joins L2's ordering question to a concrete kind
of ecological memory.

There is no need to return to Q-012's fitting machinery to explore this.
Its baseline parameter belongs to a particular conditional lineage model;
it is not an observed biosphere-wide measure of maturation. The conceptual
question can develop without borrowing that interpretation.

## 3. Evolutionary opportunities may have opening hours

Claude's H1 places viable body plans in a network of developmental
reachability. His K branch adds changing physical limits. C1 adds the
possibility that a trait only works when another organism or ecological
process exists. Put them together and the opportunity can be temporary.

Imagine an innovation requiring three conditions at once:

- a developmental route from an existing lineage;
- an environment in which the resulting organism can function;
- a partner, resource pathway or ecological setting that makes it useful.

Each condition might exist for a long time, but their overlap could be brief.
The innovation might appear abruptly when the overlap begins, or never appear
because the lineage reaches the developmental route after the environment has
changed. The unoccupied part of morphospace could include forms that were
viable and reachable at different times, but never simultaneously.

**Could some apparent bursts of evolvability be moments when several clocks
align?** That adds a rival to an internal developmental threshold without
rejecting H1. It also changes the interpretation of H2's "rested" and "tired"
lineages: an old lineage might retain an unused capacity whose ecological
conditions have not yet arrived. Its eventual radiation would not require
evolvability to have regenerated after exhaustion.

M's rotation and tidal prompts suggest an even stranger extension. Periodic
windows for activity, development, resource supply or dispersal could coincide
or drift apart. Might changing the alignment of such windows open a way of
life while leaving annual energy input or mean conditions similar? This is
a question about timing, not a claim that a particular Phanerozoic innovation
was caused by day length.

An imagined history could therefore contain a **missed radiation**: all its
necessary ingredients existed somewhere in the world, but never met at the
right time. A different ordering of ordinary changes might have produced a
very different biosphere without a miraculous new trait.

## 4. Can ecological instructions travel without their original carriers?

J2's drainage basins give G2's ecological reproduction a concrete setting.
Instead of asking only whether descendant basins share species, imagine
asking what else a basin can pass on when it splits, captures another river
or exports colonists.

A daughter system might inherit organisms, the order in which ecological
roles become available, a constructed habitat, or a set of dependencies.
Those forms of inheritance need not have equal lifetimes. Eventually every
species could change while something about the organisation remains.

**Could a small founding combination reconstruct a much larger ecological
organisation?** Imagine a consortium that builds or modifies a setting in
ways that recruit further roles. The founders need not bring the complete
community. What spreads is a process of assembly. In another world, no such
inheritance exists: similar physical conditions repeatedly generate similar
communities from unrelated colonists.

That distinction matters for the imaginative claim. Similar organisations
arising independently would demonstrate convergence, not descent. Landscape
inheritance, biological transmission and repeated assembly under common
conditions are three different stories. The interesting possibility is that
all three occur, with different components of an organisation following
different histories.

Connecting this to C1 opens an unusual evolutionary unit: an ecological
combination whose capacity to establish itself can persist through replacement
of its members. Could such combinations themselves acquire variants? Could
they split, fuse or lose the capacity to reconstruct themselves? Drainages,
soil communities and reefs suggest different imagined settings, without
assuming that the same answer applies to all of them.

## 5. What if relationships disappear while their members survive?

Claude's symbiont-crisis idea asks whether the visible host dies because an
invisible partner fails. Turn that around. Suppose both organisms survive,
but the association does not.

**Could a world undergo a mass loss of ecological partnerships without a mass
loss of taxa?** A change in seasonal timing, geographic contact, physiological
compatibility or resource exchange could dismantle relationships while
preserving the component lineages. Taxonomic continuity might then coexist
with a profound ecological transition.

Now imagine recovery proceeding through reassortment of surviving partners.
A surge of ecological novelty might reflect new combinations rather than
new component traits. I2's hybrid-origin prompt becomes one member of a
larger family: recombination of genomes, symbioses, ecological dependencies
and constructed habitats. These are distinct processes, but asking which
level is being recombined could be more informative than calling all of
them a radiation.

This also changes D2's encounter between biotas. Contact might destroy old
compatibilities, create new ones or allow a widespread partner to connect
formerly separate systems. A partner that initially rescues many hosts could
later become a shared point of failure. Thus the same contact could increase
local persistence and synchronise vulnerability across regions.

The provocative object is **the history of viable combinations**. Species
richness is one description of its ingredients, not the whole object we
would be trying to understand.

## 6. A size distribution can remember elsewhere as well as earlier

Claude's O1 offers body size as a clock since disturbance. Add D3's regional
storage of alternatives, and a local assemblage can acquire large bodies
without evolving them again: large-bodied lineages could return from elsewhere.
Add K1, and changing physical or resource limits could change the attainable
sizes even without a long period of biological rebuilding.

This gives three different histories behind a similar local size distribution:
new evolution within the recovering system, recolonisation from surviving
source regions, and a changing limit on growth or persistence. Rather than
requiring size to function as a universal clock, ask how it combines those
histories.

**Where is the memory of a former biosphere stored?** It might remain in local
survivors, distant refuges, developmental capacities or environmental legacies.
A recovery can look slow because new capabilities must evolve, because existing
ones cannot reach the site, or because the site no longer permits them.

A compelling imagined comparison would give two depleted regions the same
environment but different access to surviving source biotas. One regains an
old organisation; the other develops an alternative. Which would later look
more "advanced", more stable or more innovative—and how much of that reading
would mistake access to stored history for an intrinsic property of the fauna?

## Two historical anchors for branch P

I checked only these two sources, on 24 October 2026, to keep the historical
premise precise. This was a targeted reading, not a novelty search or a
feasibility assessment of the new ideas. The rest of Claude's historical
lineage is not independently checked here.

- **Sakamoto, Venditti & Benton (2017), DOI
  [10.1111/2041-210X.12666](https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210X.12666).**
  Read the abstract and Introduction of the
  [accepted manuscript](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/ws/files/106212913/Sakamoto_2016_MEE.pdf),
  particularly manuscript lines 58–88. Their central criticism concerns
  the SDDM procedure that independently sorts diversity and its sampling
  predictor, destroying their original pairing. It should not be compressed
  into a claim that every regression-based treatment of sampling was tested
  and refuted.
- **Hannisdal & Peters (2011), DOI
  [10.1126/science.1210695](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1210695).**
  Read the abstract and analytical description on pp. 1121–1122 of the
  [author-hosted paper](https://strata.geology.wisc.edu/reprints/Hannisdal_Peters2011.pdf).
  They examine conditional and directional information transfer among
  environmental, rock and diversity records. The field has already attempted
  to go beyond simple correlations. We can develop a different source of
  leverage without treating that work as absent or its causal interpretation
  as settled.
