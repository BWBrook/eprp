## Make room for the idea before asking it to defend itself

The [first blue-sky format](register-blue-sky.md#format) required a prior-work
check, observation model, accessible route and discriminating test at entry.
Those belong before research investment. For this round, they come **after
exploration**. The recent Q-012/Q-038 work made technical qualification the
centre of our exchanges; it need not dictate what we imagine next.

I propose three movements, with the first two protected from ranking:

1. **Throw ideas into the map.** Questions, mechanisms, analogies, apparent
   contradictions and imagined worlds are all welcome. An idea need not yet
   have data, an estimator or a polished formulation. Mark an uncertain
   premise as uncertain and continue the thought.
2. **Change and connect them together.** Extend the other's idea before
   critiquing it; invert a causal arrow, swap a scale or organism, remove a
   familiar assumption, or connect distant branches. Preserve several
   incompatible versions when they lead somewhere interesting.
3. **Then scrutinise.** First ask what would change in our understanding if
   the idea were right or wrong. Next check nearby literature, observation
   and simulation routes, and what would distinguish it from a rival. Keep
   scientific interest separate from present tractability. Some ideas will
   be discarded; a few may remain compelling without a current route.

The immediate product is a landscape we can think with. No forced shortlist,
new register IDs, prior-search quota or simulation campaign. Our first
contributions need not be independent in order to be useful: this is a
conversation, and cross-fertilisation is the point. Claude can start with
unprompted branches before reading mine if that helps avoid anchoring.

### A. Memory and unrealised futures

**A1 — Could the same Earth support several very different biospheres?**
Imagine matching climate, continental arrangement and external nutrient
supply, but changing the order in which ecological capabilities appeared.
Could the resulting worlds remain different for tens of millions of years?
The exciting possibility is that history changes what is stably possible,
not merely which names occupy equivalent roles.

**A2 — Can diversity recover while evolutionary possibilities remain lost?**
An extinction might remove a developmental route, ecological partnership or
environmental constructor that made whole families of subsequent innovations
accessible. A species-rich recovery could then occupy a smaller set of
possible futures. What would such a loss mean, and could it ever be inferred?

**A3 — Can an ecosystem inherit a world built by organisms that are gone?**
Suppose soils, substrates, sediment structure or chemical reservoirs retain
the effects of former occupants. Later communities might be assembled partly
by extinct organisms' legacies. How long could that ecological inheritance
persist, and could it create recovery without recreating the former builders?

### B. Organisms change the rules

**B1 — Can successful nutrient capture eventually impoverish a biosphere?**
Imagine innovations that retain limiting elements increasingly well within
organisms, partnerships or sediments. Local advantage might reduce resource
circulation elsewhere. Could evolutionary success create its own resource
constraint, and could disruption release a pulse of opportunities?

**B2 — Are some major transitions changes in energy transport?**
What if the decisive innovation is a new way to connect production and
consumption across habitats: sinking material, migrating organisms, rooting
systems or mobile consumers? The available energy need not increase for its
geographic distribution and ecological consequences to change profoundly.

**B3 — Can life change the variability against which it evolves?**
Imagine vegetation, reef systems or sediment communities buffering some
fluctuations while amplifying others. Evolution would then change the
frequency, persistence and spatial coherence of its own selective pressures.
Could a biosphere become increasingly stable locally yet more vulnerable
to a shared failure?

### C. Missing partners and invisible actors

**C1 — What if some innovations belong to communities, not lineages?**
An apparently useful innovation might have little value until a partner,
substrate maker, disperser or resource processor exists. Several lineages
could therefore unlock an opportunity jointly. Could long delays and sudden
expansions reflect assembly of such combinations rather than difficulty
evolving the focal trait?

**C2 — Could hidden antagonists organise conspicuous fossil patterns?**
Imagine parasites, pathogens or parasitoids setting limits on abundance,
geographic contact or ecological dominance while leaving little direct fossil
evidence. What larger-scale signatures could emerge in their visible hosts?
This opens a branch especially worth exploring for insects and terrestrial
communities, without assuming a usable proxy already exists.

**C3 — Could microbial ecological changes repeatedly redefine animal opportunity?**
Keep atmospheric oxygen broadly similar but change recycling, symbiosis,
nutrient accessibility or the fate of organic matter. Could very different
animal and plant worlds follow? The provocation is to treat poorly observed
microbial states as possible organisers, rather than a fixed background.

### D. Geography as an evolutionary process

**D1 — Does the rhythm of joining and separating habitats matter beyond area?**
Two worlds could have the same average habitat area and connectivity but
different sequences of isolation and contact. One might repeatedly generate
and then combine ecological experiments; the other might erase them too
quickly. Could the timing itself change the production of major innovations?

**D2 — Can two flourishing ecosystems be incompatible when they meet?**
Bring together communities that are each internally persistent. Their
interaction might disrupt nutrient cycles, partners or ecological control,
even without a universally superior competitor. Could some deep-time
replacements be failures of compatibility between systems?

**D3 — Does provinciality preserve alternative ecological futures?**
Geographically separate regions might maintain different solutions to the
same problem. Mixing could spread useful combinations, but could also erase
that diversity of solutions. When would a connected planet gain local
richness while losing its capacity to reorganise after a later disturbance?

### E. Perception, behaviour and biological organisation

**E1 — Could a new sensory world reorganise ecosystems without a new body plan?**
Changes in what organisms detect, signal or conceal might alter available
prey, partners and refuges. Imagine holding much external morphology fixed
while changing the effective range of vision, chemical detection or acoustic
communication. Could an ecological transition precede the anatomical one
we usually look for?

**E2 — Can behavioural innovations change the planet before skeletons change?**
Feeding, movement, shelter building and social behaviour might alter
sediments, vegetation or material transport before obvious morphological
divergence. Could traces and environmental change reveal a transition that
a body-fossil account would date much later?

**E3 — When can a collective achieve what an individual cannot?**
Imagine colonial, modular, social or symbiotic organisation reaching an
ecological capability without evolving the equivalent within one body.
Could different forms of organisation be alternative routes through the
same constraint? Plants, insects and colonial marine organisms might supply
very different ways to think about this, rather than just more examples of
one animal-centred story.

### F. Clocks, signatures and the record life makes

**F1 — Could environmental rhythms select different worlds at the same mean and variance?**
Change the ordering and frequency of disturbance while preserving its overall
distribution. If generation, dispersal, ecosystem construction and recovery
operate on different timescales, the winners and accessible innovations might
change. Could repeated crises sometimes reveal a mismatch of clocks rather
than exceptional disturbance magnitude?

**F2 — What is the right clock for a macroevolutionary comparison?**
Equal millions of years need not represent equal generations, population
turnovers, occupied habitat histories or energy throughput. Might some
apparently changing evolutionary rates become intelligible in another
biological currency? Or would failure to collapse them reveal a real change
in the organisation of evolution?

**F3 — Could evolution rewrite its own apparent tempo by changing rock formation?**
Suppose evolving organisms change sediment production, disturbance,
mineralisation and preservation together. The biological process and the
archive would then coevolve. Could apparently abrupt transitions contain
both a real ecological change and a change in how that change becomes
visible? The scientific question is the coupled system, beyond correcting
one sampling bias.

### G. Deliberately awkward propositions

**G1 — Could a biosphere age even if its constituent lineages do not?**
Imagine ecological dependencies accumulating until nearly every successful
change requires several other things to change first. With external forcing
held constant, might a world become progressively less able to discover new
ways of living? "Ageing" is a provocation here, not an established mechanism
or an assumption that an extinction would rejuvenate anything.

**G2 — Could ecological organisations reproduce?**
Imagine a bundle of relationships spreading between regions, retaining its
organisation while replacing all its constituent species. Could such bundles
have recognisable descent, variation and differential persistence? That would
make the history of life partly a history of organisations travelling through
changing lineages. Whether this is more than an analogy is a later question.

**G3 — What forms of life are physically possible but historically unreachable?**
Imagine mapping viable organisms or communities without requiring an
evolutionary path from the starting state. The gap between viable and
reachable worlds might tell us something different from the occupied fossil
morphospace. Could extinction, partnership or environmental construction open
paths into those worlds—or close them permanently?

## Connections that might make something less familiar

| Connect | New possibility to play with |
| --- | --- |
| A2 lost futures × C1 missing partners | A recovery restores the component lineages but misses the combinations that once made innovation possible. |
| A3 inheritance × B1 nutrient capture | Extinct builders keep regulating resources; a later release could look like a delayed evolutionary response. |
| C2 hidden antagonists × D2 ecosystem contact | Contact reorganises communities through unfamiliar antagonists, without direct competitive superiority of the incoming visible taxa. |
| D1 geography × F1 biological clocks | The tempo of continental contact determines whether ecological combinations can form before isolation breaks them apart. |
| E1 sensory worlds × E3 collective organisation | A collective sensing or signalling system opens an ecological role no solitary organism can occupy. |
| B2 energy transport × F3 self-made archive | The innovation that reorganises energy flow also changes where evidence accumulates, producing a coupled biological and stratigraphic transition. |
| G1 ageing × G2 ecological reproduction | A spreading ecological organisation could carry accumulated constraints into a new region rather than begin a fresh evolutionary experiment. |
| G3 unreachable worlds × C1 missing partners | A viable innovation may be inaccessible to any lineage evolving alone, yet accessible through an evolving partnership. |

These connections are invitations, not claims that the joined mechanisms
occurred. Some premises may fail immediately once we examine them. The first
job is to find the strongest, most interesting version of each thought.
