The most productive expansion is to ask how organisms acquire the conditions
needed to live, reproduce and establish elsewhere. Some conditions are
physically present, some are supplied by other organisms, some must be detected,
and some must occur at a particular stage. Evolution can change all four.
This is a collection of connected questions, not a claim that they share one
mechanism or that their constituent mechanisms are new.

I keep both dependence and sensory access open. Predator escalation belongs
within the latter, but recruitment, developmental induction, predictive cues
and the costs of sensing extend beyond B-003. The strongest additions concern
capabilities that exist but cannot establish, dependence that enables dispersal,
and environments that remain tolerable while becoming poorly predicted.

## 1. Does dependence remove a capability, or prevent its use?

Claude's dependence ratchet asks whether long stable intervals let lineages
lose costly autonomous functions until they can no longer survive without
providers. The important historical question is whether dependence becomes
harder to reverse with time. Loss of a function, current reliance on another
organism, and failure to restore function after interruption must be separated.

Consider four imagined communities with the same present service output and
the same dominant providers:

| Community | What changes when providers disappear? | What would distinguish it? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Autonomous capacity has been lost | Recovery needs innovation, immigration or a new association | No sufficient capacity among surviving residents, under the tested conditions |
| Capacity survives in rare variants | Existing variants increase after provider loss | Rescue begins from detectable standing variants before new acquisition is required |
| Capacity is retained but suppressed | Residents alter expression or behaviour | The same individuals restore provision without replacement |
| A different process can substitute | The original function is unnecessary in its former form | Persistence returns through a different pathway, or because demand changes |

The second world has a concrete prior. In Meijer, van Dijk & Hogeweg's (2020)
virtual microbial communities, autonomous mutants could arise in cross-feeding
systems without displacing residents. After partner removal, standing
autonomous variants could take over in assays with further mutation disabled.
That is ecological release of existing capacity, not new adaptation generated
by the interruption. It is a simulation result, not evidence for a deep-time
trend. [Primary study](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42003-020-1107-x).

**Could ordinary competition suppress the very variants that would permit
recovery after a rare disruption?** Selection for present performance could
reduce the abundance of autonomous alternatives without eliminating their
possibility. Conversely, tiny reserves may suffice when they can expand before
dependants die. The important comparison is between the abundance and expansion
time of alternatives, the duration of remaining service, and the time until
dependants fail. A count of provider taxa alone cannot decide the outcome.

This sharpens the ratchet into a question about *recoverability*. Does time
spent dependent reduce the probability of recovering function, conditional on
measured present composition and capacity? Or do apparently irreversible communities
remain capable of rapid reorganisation? Meijer's mechanism is already occupied;
a further contribution would need to explain variation in the persistence of
those alternatives, or establish a different historical consequence.

### The coral lead does not presently support the proposed ratchet

Barbeitos, Romano & Lasker (2010) inferred repeated losses of coloniality and
photosymbiosis. However, their separate-trait analyses strongly favoured equal
forward and reverse transition rates; the ancestral root was uncertain. The
paper does not establish that gains are rarer than losses.
[Primary study](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0914380107).

Navarro Campoy et al. (2020), using 513 species rather than 80, preferred a
correlated episodic model and inferred repeated origins but very infrequent
loss of photosymbiosis. This is a conflicting reconstruction, with its own
sampling, dating and model limits. Neither analysis measures host metabolic
autonomy or the concentration of essential provision.
[Primary study](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmars.2020.00461).

There is also a mismatch between the trait and the question. A coral losing
photosymbiosis has lost a partnership; that does not show it has lost an
autonomous function. The relevant evidence would be its performance without
the partner, retained alternative nutrition and routes to reacquisition.
Presence or absence of a partnership is insufficient to establish the ratchet.

## 2. Can increasing dependence enable independence at another level?

Imagine a lineage that acquires a partner supplying a limiting resource.
Initially, establishment requires encountering that partner at every new
location. Later, both disperse together and the association reproduces
reliably. Each member remains dependent, but the combination can establish
where neither could establish alone.

**When does co-transmission of partners expand geographical or ecological
opportunity more than specialisation restricts it?** The proposed comparison
is among autonomous organisms, associations assembled from the environment,
and associations transmitted together. Hold the combined metabolic capabilities
fixed initially, so that the effect of transmission can be distinguished from
acquiring an additional function.

Three contrary outcomes are plausible. Joint transmission removes a recurrent
assembly problem and permits colonisation. Alternatively, it carries an
unsuitable partner into every destination and prevents beneficial replacement.
Or joint failure makes the association less robust than partners that can
recombine freely. Which outcome prevails could depend on spatial variation in
partner compatibility and on how often environments change after establishment.

This develops E3, collective capability, and the earlier reassembly question.
The combination's establishment probability need not equal either member's
establishment probability when dispersed separately. It also challenges a
simple biosphere-ageing interpretation:
loss of autonomy at one organisational level could accompany greater
reproducibility and opportunity at another. This does not by itself establish
a new evolutionary individual, aligned interests or selection at the group
level. Those are additional propositions.

The broad terrain overlaps symbiosis, transmission evolution and major
transitions. No nearest-prior search of this branch was performed here. The
specific question to retain is whether **the evolution of how partners travel
changes the set of places they can establish**, independently of improvements
in their combined physiology.

## 3. Can an environment remain tolerable while becoming misleading?

Sensory access is about more than detection distance. Organisms also use present
conditions to anticipate what will happen during development, breeding,
dormancy or migration. The cue can remain detectable while ceasing to predict
the condition that matters.

Imagine environmental reorganisation that changes the association between a
seasonal cue and a resource pulse. Neither variable needs acquire a new mean,
variance or extreme. A previously effective response can nevertheless become
poorly timed. Improved detection of the same cue would not solve that problem.

A transparent construction makes the distinction precise. Let a cue C and
the subsequently favoured phenotype T each take values -1 and +1 equally
often. An organism responds with phenotype z=C. In one world C=T with
probability 0.9; in another, with probability 0.1. Both marginals are unchanged,
but expected squared mismatch rises from 0.4 to 3.6. A nonresponsive z=0 has
mismatch 1 in both worlds. These are hypothetical performance costs, not
extinction probabilities. Changing the response to z=-C would recover the
original mismatch in the second world.

**Could some changes in extinction selectivity or radiation reflect changes
in which cues are useful, rather than changes in physiological tolerance?**
One lineage may rely on a distant predictor; another samples the relevant
condition directly; a third distributes reproduction across times or places.
Their ranking can change when predictability changes, even at the same
distribution of stressful conditions.

This mechanism has substantial prior treatment. Chevin & Lande (2015) model
evolving responses to multiple correlated environmental cues, including
indirect indicators that do not themselves impose selection. Only their
abstract was read here; it establishes adjacency, not a completed novelty
assessment. [Primary theoretical paper](https://doi.org/10.1111/evo.12755).

The extension worth discussing is the macroevolutionary consequence of
different cue systems and prediction horizons. Its strongest rivals are
ordinary exposure differences, physiological tolerance and demographic delay.
A theoretical comparison could hold environmental marginals and tolerance
fixed while changing cue–outcome relationships, then allow cue switching,
plasticity and bet hedging. Fossils will seldom identify the decision rule
directly; this remains a theoretical opportunity rather than a promised fossil
test. It also develops F1: matching the distribution of environmental values
does not match the biological information they contain.

## 4. Can dependence be concentrated in a brief transition rather than adult life?

Suppose adult organisms can live in a place, food and substrate are available,
and propagules arrive, but a biological input needed for attachment or
metamorphosis is absent. Existing adults might persist while establishment of
a new generation fails. The provider could be inconspicuous and matter only
for a short interval.

**Could evolutionary changes in developmental induction reorganise accessible
habitats without changing adult tolerance?** This links dependence and sensory
access, but the underlying mechanisms must remain distinct. An organism may
use a cue as information about habitat quality, or it may require an external
biochemical action to complete development. Calling both “information” would
conceal the scientific difference.

Two primary examples make that distinction useful:

- Tebben et al. (2015) induced coral attachment and metamorphosis using
  crustose-coralline-algal extract fractions in flowing-water assays. A bacterial
  compound, TBP, could instead induce metamorphosis without attachment.
  Developmental change, settlement and subsequent recruitment are therefore
  different outcomes. The study does not establish that the treatments predict
  juvenile fitness. [Primary study](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep10803).
- Ericson, Eisenstein et al. (2019) identified a bacterial system that delivers
  a proteinaceous effector inducing tubeworm metamorphosis. Purified effector
  supplied externally failed, whereas intracellular delivery induced the
  transition. This is mechanistic evidence for developmental induction; it
  does not demonstrate assessment of habitat quality.
  [Primary study](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.46845).

The discriminating comparison crosses actual habitat suitability with the
presence and identity of the inducing input. Separate approach, attachment,
metamorphosis, juvenile performance and adult reproduction. A developmental
bypass followed by normal survival would identify a missing transition
requirement, but would not alone prove informational use.

The expansive question is whether diversification creates dependence on a
small set of transition-enabling organisms even when adult communities appear
functionally redundant. A contrary world has many interchangeable inducers or
autonomous development, so loss of particular providers barely matters.
Another has ordinary substrate or food deterioration that explains both
provider loss and recruitment failure. Fossil co-occurrence cannot distinguish
these explanations unaided. Existing experiments and theory can establish
what patterns would be worth seeking before proposing a deep-time case.

## 5. When does obtaining information make an organism more vulnerable?

Better sensing need not mean greater opportunity. Active sensing can reveal
the sender, and attention to one task can reduce performance on another.
These are different constraints: the first concerns information emitted to
others, the second information processed by the organism.

Poon & Crampton (2026) report electric-signal suppression by electric eels and
their knifefish prey, with field observations and laboratory playbacks.
This provides an example of context-dependent withdrawal from active sensing.
The available text does not establish a measured evolutionary advantage;
passive sensing during silence is an interpretation. Full-text access was
limited. [Primary study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2026.02.026).

**Could access to a habitat depend on when sensing can be used safely, even
after the sensory machinery has evolved?** Rivals include energy conservation,
interference and general stress responses. The informative contrast separates
the usefulness of sensing from how detectable it makes the animal. The
resulting evolutionary question concerns conditional use, alternative sensory
channels and activity schedules, not a single ranking of sensory power.

A separate expansion concerns attention. In controlled blue-jay experiments,
Dukas & Kamil (2000) found that a difficult central search task reduced
detection of peripheral targets. They used rewarded images, not attacks by
predators. Their 2001 study found poorer simultaneous detection of two prey
types than focused search, without the predicted penalty for switching between
types. These constrain a simple claim that more stimuli or more switching
always impairs detection. [2000 experiment](https://doi.org/10.1093/beheco/11.5.502);
[2001 experiment](https://doi.org/10.1093/beheco/12.2.192).

**Can diversification change the perceptual conditions that permit further
diversification?** More distinct prey appearances might reduce detection of
rare forms under limited attention, whereas a common shared appearance might
increase detection through learning. Predator switching, specialist predators,
energetic benefits of abundant prey and multisensory compensation could erase
or reverse that effect. Existing work on search images and frequency-dependent
predation occupies the basic mechanism. The larger question is whether it
produces sustained differences in community composition or accessible forms,
rather than merely short-term changes in capture rate. We have not established
that macroevolutionary extension.

## 6. What these additions change in the sensory argument

The sensory branch now contains detection, predictive use of cues,
developmental induction, and the costs of obtaining or processing information.
They should be compared without being collapsed into a general information
index. B-003 can receive the predator–prey escalation connection while the
other questions remain available to the wider programme.

Three of Claude's proposed collapse conditions need narrowing:

- A shorter locomotor range than detection range does not make sensing
  irrelevant: detection can change route choice, avoidance and allocation of
  movement within that range. A strict inaccessible-patch example is narrower.
- Rapid counter-adaptation need not restore the former ecology. Burrowing,
  nocturnality or a shift to deeper water can leave persistent changes in
  resource use, encounter rates and later evolutionary opportunities.
- Environmental visibility changes need not affect all visual guilds equally;
  optics, depth, spectral sensitivity and behaviour differ. Conversely, an
  innovation can affect competitors and partners as well as its immediate
  prey. Those patterns require quantitative expectations before serving as
  discriminators.

These are qualifications that broaden the possibilities. They do not turn
every behavioural change into an evolutionary explanation.

## 7. The useful order-null correction, and two limits on the ratchet argument

### Regrowth and persistence

I accept Claude's correction and have amended my earlier note. Fixed per-type
growth multipliers commute with fixed survival multipliers, so they preserve
order invariance of expected abundance. Density-dependent growth need not.
For example, with g(n)=2n/(1+n), n=1, p=1/2 and q=4/5, qg(pn)=8/15,
whereas pg(qn)=4/9. This is an ordinary saturating-growth example, not acquired
damage or adaptation.

Claude's founder example is also correct. With independent individual survival,
one founder, p=1/2, q=1 and ten descendants per surviving founder, extinction
probabilities are 1/2 and 1/1024 in the two orders; expected abundance is 5
in either. Expected abundance and persistence are different targets. For N
independent founders, deterministic r descendants and shock survival p and q:

```text
P(extinction | A then B) = [1 - p + p(1 - q)^r]^N
P(extinction | B then A) = [1 - q + q(1 - p)^r]^N.
```

For interior probabilities both approach zero as N grows. But abundance is
not a count of independently exposed units. Correlated environmental losses,
spatial concentration and positive-density thresholds can defeat the proposed
generalisation to widespread taxa. Even here, absolute order differences need
not decrease monotonically from N=1: with very low survival both sequences
initially fail almost surely. The rare-lineage pattern is a conditional
demographic prediction, not a unique diagnostic.

Likewise, additive input, type change or nonlinearity *can* produce an order
effect; merely including one does not guarantee it. Symmetry and cancellation
can preserve equality. The five processes are useful alternatives to analyse,
not an exhaustive or mutually exclusive classification of observed patterns.

### Gain rates, loss rates and loss of provision

A greater gain rate than loss rate is not a sufficient collapse condition for
the ratchet. Rates act on different numbers of eligible lineages. In a fixed
collection with autonomous fraction u, gain rate g per dependent lineage and
loss rate l per autonomous lineage:

```text
du/dt = g(1 - u) - l u.
```

With 90 autonomous and 10 dependent lineages, g=0.2 and l=0.1 imply an
initial net loss of seven autonomous lineages per time unit in expectation,
despite the higher gain rate. The corresponding fraction tends toward 2/3,
not zero. Branching, differential extinction, immigration and functional
substitution add further terms. This toy model makes no empirical rate claim.
It shows why a rate asymmetry alone establishes neither irreversible decline
nor its absence.

Provider correlation is also not the earlier Cov(p,q). That covariance is
across types' probabilities in two different stresses. Correlated survival or
failure outcomes among providers under one stress are another dependence structure; their presence
does not imply a negative cross-stress covariance. Response diversity matters
alongside absolute tolerances, provider abundance, demand, alternative delivery
routes and recovery time. Frequency dependence may rescue a synchronously
reduced provider population if survivors and time remain. Conversely, gradual
decline can fail if provision crosses a threshold before recovery.

All numerical constructions above were checked with exact fractions. They
are counterexamples and restricted nulls, not fitted models or fossil results.
