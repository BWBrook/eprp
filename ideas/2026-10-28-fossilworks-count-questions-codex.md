# What Fossilworks counts could reveal about the reorganisation of communities

28 October 2026. Barry asked for distinctive questions enabled by the
Fossilworks counts and for the strongest candidates to be added to the EPRP
register. This note is an **ideation and bounded feasibility check**, not a
primary analysis, a novelty verdict, or a claim that specimen counts directly
measure living populations. Two candidates, [Q-051 and Q-052](register.md#fossilworks-count-enabled-candidates-28-october), are
under review. Both can be investigated without assuming the late-Quaternary
changes had one cause.

## Why counts add something

A presence–absence matrix can say that two surviving species were recorded
before and after a transition. It cannot say whether one became relatively
common in the recovered remains and the other rare. Nor can it tell whether
geographically separated assemblages contain the same dominants when their lists
of species look similar. Those contrasts matter because the taxonomic identity
of a community and the distribution of its recorded specimens can change
independently.

The distinction has a crucial mathematical limit. Suppose a pre-transition
assemblage contains 40 specimens of A, 10 of B and 50 of an extinct species X;
afterwards it contains 40 A, 10 B and no X. A's share doubles from 40% to 80%,
but the A:B ratio remains 4:1. The share increase alone is a consequence of
removing X, even though neither surviving count changed. In real fossil data,
changes in mortality, skeletal production, excavation, deposition and
preservation add further uncertainty: even an altered A:B specimen ratio
need not mean an altered ratio of living animals. Ratios remove a shared
sampling multiplier, but not changes in taxon-specific mortality or recovery.

### 1. Survivor reorganisation beyond loss: Q-051

**Question.** In well-dated and comparably accumulated assemblages, did the
relative representation of surviving mammals change across a major faunal
transition more than expected from deleting locally or globally lost taxa?
Did the change differ from comparable background intervals?

**Why it matters.** A crisis may remove species while leaving the relative
representation of survivors much as before, or it may also reorder which
survivors dominate. The first pattern does not imply an intact ecosystem; the
second would reveal an additional change masked by a species list. This is a
conditional descriptive comparison, not a test that extinction caused the
reordering.

**Comparison.** Define regions, taxon treatment, independently dated windows,
accumulation contexts and a survivor set before the analysis. At matched
localities, use pre-event shares *restricted to survivors* as the deletion-only
reference, and compare these with post-event shares restricted to the same
survivors. Survivor-to-survivor log ratios make the denominator issue explicit.
Compare with within-period and background-interval changes using the same
observation model. Newly appearing species and taxa with ambiguous survival or
identification are reported separately; the treatment of nondetection must be
modelled rather than silently equated to absence. The shared-survivor outcome
is not the total impact of the transition. Conditioning on taxa recovered on
both sides can itself select the most detectable species, so sensitivity to
that selection is part of the design.

A local shift and a geographically consistent shift are different outcomes.
For example, one survivor might gain relative to another in every habitat, or
only where the latter's habitat contracts. Include spatial and habitat structure
and independent background sequences. Fix a scientifically meaningful effect
and uncertainty target before fitting; an imprecise comparison is not a null.
**Strong rival:** taxon-specific preservation, skeletal representation,
archaeological choice or chronological averaging changes along with time. Site
or publication effects cannot identify an ecological change if the collection
protocol changes perfectly with time. If realistic observation models can
reproduce the apparent signal, park the biological interpretation.

**Nearest work and residual.** Tóth et al. 2019 already examine changed
co-occurrence *among surviving mammals* through the end-Pleistocene extinction;
Carter and Alroy 2025 analyse count-weighted mass and energetic proxies across
pre/post assemblages from the same database family. Strotz and Lieberman 2021
compare taxonomic and relative-abundance stability in fossil marine
communities. Our narrower proposed contrast is the survivor-conditional
specimen reallocation beyond deletion, with background and observation
controls; its novelty against the complete literature remains unverified.
The count comparison does not by itself identify competition, release, biomass,
energetic compensation or interaction networks. Q-028 predicts traits of
post-crisis winners; this candidate asks how the observed share of survivors
changes. Q-009 concerns consequences for later community behaviour, which
would require an additional independent outcome.

### 2. Do widespread survivors remain widespread dominants? Q-052

**Question.** When faunal lists become more alike or remain regionally
distinct, do the *identities and relative specimen shares* of their local
common species change in the same way? In particular, can surviving taxa
remain recorded across a broad region while their relative dominance becomes
concentrated in fewer habitats or locations?

**Why it matters.** Two fossil assemblages may retain many of the same species
but have entirely different relative shares. Conversely, a few common species
may recur across otherwise distinctive lists. Either result would change what
we mean by biotic homogenisation. Broad occurrence is also not proof that a
taxon remained common across its range; concentration would suggest a
hypothesis about ecological contraction, not establish a demographic refuge.

**Comparison.** Compare geographically replicated, protocol-comparable fossil
assemblages within two independently dated periods, using the same taxonomic
and spatial domain. Analyse incidence similarity and an explicit separate
outcome for the similarity of relative shares of common survivors; record the
incidence matrix and count support on which each rests. A useful conditional
contrast renormalises over the species shared by each eligible site pair, with
at least two shared taxa and sufficient detection, and compares relative-share
profiles against a null generated under observed incidence, sample size,
taxon-specific baseline shares and a model for differential recovery. Also
estimate the geographic or environmental spread of a focal taxon's high
relative share conditional on recorded occupancy. For example, a prespecified
endpoint could be the proportion of sites recording a focal taxon where it
supplies at least 20% of counted specimens among a fixed, independently defined
survivor set, alongside sensitivity to that threshold. The fixed survivor
denominator controls the direct effect of removing extinct taxa: for example,
A=20, B=80 and extinct X=100 puts A below 20% of the total before X is lost
but at 20% afterwards, although A:B stays fixed. This illustrative threshold
is not an established biological boundary. The
proportion is a *frequency* of relative dominance, not a measure of spatial
concentration: for that, also estimate the spatial dispersion or spread across
prespecified environmental classes of high-share sites, with a fixed sampling
footprint and a null conditioned on their number. Pair-specific denominators,
common-species selection and shared sites require sensitivity checks and
resampling by independent site/region and source, not by every pair. Fix a
consequential change and a sampling-informed baseline before fitting. Report
negligible effects only with sufficient precision.

Do not subtract a raw Jaccard score from a raw Bray–Curtis score and call the
difference an ecological effect: their scales and sampling responses differ.
Use counterfactual simulations calibrated to the observation process and
report both outcomes. Analyse human-accumulated and nonhuman-accumulated
assemblages separately before considering synthesis; shared procurement
practices can make refuse assemblages look similar even if wild populations do
not. Unknown zeroes, spatial imbalance, age uncertainty and changes in fossil
formation remain central. Count-based comparisons cannot establish absolute
animal density or a process of homogenisation by humans.

**Nearest work and residual.** Fraser et al. 2022 already document increasing
North American mammal assemblage similarity using occurrence-based Jaccard and
other incidence metrics. Brook et al. 2025 already identify global
Pleistocene–Holocene community regions and use Jaccard for 34 approximately
paired locations; those are candidate locations, not 34 validated count-based
pairs. Carter and Alroy 2025 discuss geographic variation in count-weighted
energetics, including domesticates. Brocklehurst et al. 2018 show that
frequency distributions affect fossil beta-diversity estimation even with
sampling standardisation. The residual is a direct contrast of occurrence
structure with survivor dominance in the same source-vetted geographical
panel; neither a weighted-distance calculation nor a repeat of generic
homogenisation is an adequate contribution. Link B-015 (macroecological
distributions), Q-025 (local versus global loss) and Q-028 (post-crisis winners).

## What the retained data support now

A small, reproducible [profile](../data/fossilworks-ideation-20261028/README.md)
of the hash-pinned assembly and annotation sidecar counts 1,675 exact sample
IDs in 1,697 summary rows. The 22 repeated summaries have identical fields
apart from source-row ID; this is an export observation, not proof that all
other records are biologically independent. There are 359 Holocene and 336
Late Pleistocene sample IDs. The median sum of register counts per sample is
140 (interquartile range 40–537), but these are **sums of potentially mixed
units**, not individuals; provider-reported richness has median 12 (7–20).
For a deeper-time alternative, 23 French Early Miocene rodent-bearing sample
IDs are associated with 14 references, and 16 Spanish Middle Miocene IDs with
11 references. These are raw lead counts, not protocol-compatible or independent
replicates, and they do not yet constitute a before/after panel. The database
also records 22 Maastrichtian and four Early Palaeocene sample IDs labelled
`Hell Creek`, an initial search lead rather than an independently verified or
balanced K–Pg series. The whole fossil download contains Cretaceous and
Cenozoic records, not uniform coverage of deep time.

Chronology and dependence require scrutiny before any effective sample-size
claim. Of 1,675 samples, 291 lack one or both numerical age bounds and one has
reversed bounds. Of 1,263 within-reference pairs with valid numerical bounds,
702 strictly overlap; 561 pairs have identical bounds, including zero-width
cases (these two counts are not disjoint). Bounds are not deposition-duration
estimates, and equal or overlapping bounds do not establish simultaneous
accumulation. Across the snapshot, 935 sample IDs are associated with 287
references that each cover multiple samples; 696 IDs share exact coordinate
strings in 232 groups. The units of independent replication must be established
from sources, not these counts.

Most important, the additive count overlay has only 58 row-level unit
assignments from 66 reviewed rows: 26 primary-publication checks at two samples,
and 40 provider-comment checks. The remaining 25,988 rows have unknown units
*under this bounded review*. Mixed units occur within both primary-checked
samples. Screening for biologically comparable specimens requires original
source work, not a global assumption that every count is NISP. Sampling
metadata is useful for finding candidates, not proof of comparability.

One caution is particularly concrete. Fossilworks reference 3701 points to
Gaffney et al.'s New Guinea cave studies. Their original paper reports that
Yuku's stratigraphy is disturbed, so its faunal trends cannot simply be
interpreted at millennial resolution; its Kiowa comparison also records hand
collection and a selected skeletal assemblage. These records are leads for
source checking, not ready-made controls. Likewise Faith 2013 describes changes
in human, carnivore and raptor accumulation in the Boomplaas sequence. A
single cave's labelled stages or a shared reference do not establish a stable
observation process.

## What not to promote as a new stand-alone question

- **Does rarity predict extinction?** Casey, Saupe and Lieberman 2021 already
  compared abundance and range as predictors in 164 brachiopod species and
  reported abundance decline before extinction without range contraction.
  Longrich et al. 2016 already compare mammalian abundance, range, extinction
  and sampling near the K–Pg boundary. Trubovitz et al. 2023 report little
  abundance–longevity relationship in radiolarians. A forward-frozen,
  detection-aware terrestrial-mammal comparison could strengthen Q-016, but
  the general relationship is occupied. The current resource screen does not
  establish enough independent temporal sequences for it.
- **Are rank-abundance shapes stable?** Alroy 2015 fitted distributions to
  1,055 modern terrestrial inventories; Redman et al. 2023 examined a
  Quaternary cave sequence. Brocklehurst, Allen and Dunne's 2023 preprint
  analyses Carboniferous–Eocene terrestrial tetrapod relative-abundance
  distributions and reports broadly stable shapes. This is close to B-015;
  neither a better fit nor a stable shape identifies ecological stability or
  competition. The existing B-015 owner retains that entry.
- **Did energy or biomass change across the end-Pleistocene?** Carter and
  Alroy 2025 have already posed this directly using the same database family.
  Their study-specific selection and treatment must be checked before drawing
  conclusions about it from our separate snapshot's mixed protocols.

An independent Codex scientific critic recommended combining the two retained
candidates into one B-015 branch and treating rarity forecasting as Q-016
support. I agree on the latter. I keep Q-051 and Q-052 as separate provisional
entries because their estimands differ: change through time among survivors at
matched localities versus geographic concentration or convergence of commonness
conditional on occupancy. Either could change while the other does not. They
share data and some observation problems, however; they are not independent
experiments or two commissions. Merge or park either if the next targeted prior
check finds its residual occupied or the source panel cannot distinguish the
proposed alternatives. No change is made to Claude-owned Q-016 or B-015.

identifying close priors for each candidate and the principal observation
