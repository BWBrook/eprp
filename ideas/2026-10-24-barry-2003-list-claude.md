## What the list says Barry cares about

Two thirds of the entries are about extinction. The list is not about
diversity curves as such. It is about **how and why lineages die, how fast,
and what that means now**. Seven threads run through it.

1. **Bridging scales, from populations to clades and from conservation to
   deep time.** This is the strongest single thread. It runs from Balmford's
   extinction filters, Tilman et al.'s extinction debt, Smith et al. and
   Pimm et al. on modern extinction rates, and Johnson on distribution,
   abundance and extinction, to McKinney's "combining ecological and
   paleontological views", Davis and May on ecology meeting palaeoecology,
   and Lee's "conservation biology meets palaeontology". PVA and demography
   papers sit beside Raup and Sepkoski.
2. **Causal debates, followed as debates.** Periodicity, impact against
   volcanism, dinosaur extinction and the Late Permian are all listed with
   their comments and replies:
   - Raup & Sepkoski against Stigler & Wagner;
   - Sheehan against Archibald and Clemens;
   - the five Late Permian replies of 1996.

   Kerr's Science news pieces chart how the field changed its mind. Arens
   on "testing hypotheses in the historical sciences" is the methodological
   pole.
3. **The statistical structure of extinction in time.** Periodicity, the
   kill curve, self-organised criticality against "no fractals"
   (Solé et al. against Kirchner & Weil), recovery lags and evolutionary
   speed limits.
4. **Whether the record can be trusted.**
   - Flessa & Jablonski on declining background extinction as an effect of
     taxonomic structure;
   - Hoffman on definitions and timescales;
   - Foote & Sepkoski on absolute completeness;
   - Benton et al. on quality through time;
   - Adrain & Westrop's empirical audit;
   - Crystal-Arens's "rocks or real?";
   - Peters & Foote on the determinants of extinction.
5. **Body size and life history as risk traits.** These get a library of
   their own, about 120 records from 2004:
   - megafaunal overkill and its rivals;
   - "death in the slow lane", where life history matters and body size does
     not;
   - size–density relationships;
   - Cope's rule;
   - maximal body size and the rareness of big, fierce animals.
6. **Recovery and origination.** Delayed recovery, the tropics as a source of
   novelty, onshore origins of higher taxa, key innovations, "evolutionary
   explosions and the phylogenetic fuse", and molecular clocks against
   fossils.
7. **Humans as an extinction cause before written history.** Hawaiian and
   Pacific island birds, moa, mammoths, and Quaternary overkill.

## Seeds from the gaps

### S1. Death in the slow lane, in deep time

**The 2003 seed.** Barry's body-mass library holds Johnson's finding for the
late-Quaternary megafauna: life history and ecology, not body size
[memory]. Its Science commentary is titled "death in the slow lane". Body
size may be a *proxy* for slow reproduction.

**The question.** Across mass and background extinctions, is the pace of
life, rather than size itself, the trait that extinctions select against?

**Why now.** Pace of life is partly measurable in fossils:
- growth increments in bivalves and brachiopods (lifespan and growth rate);
- bone histology in tetrapods;
- larval mode from the protoconch.

Post-crisis life-history shifts have been proposed, such as *Lystrosaurus*
"breeding young" after the end-Permian (Botha-Brink et al. 2016 [memory]).
Lilliput patterns are well studied for size but not separated from pace.

**Strongest rival: size itself.** Metabolic and oxygen demand and thermal
tolerance work through size directly. In physiological habitat-loss models,
size is not just a proxy.

**Overlaps.** Q-028 (winners' traits), Q-031 (shrinking under warming),
Q-038 and Q-012. S1 would add the *axis*, life-history pace, that those
entries treat only through size or taxonomy.

### S2. Novel predators as natural overkill experiments

**The 2003–04 seed.** Barry's libraries pair Quaternary overkill with the
mammals of the Great American Biotic Interchange. One example is "Testing
hypotheses of differential mammalian extinctions subsequent to the GABI".

**The question.** Before people, did the arrival of a new predator ever cause
rapid, size-selective extinction with the signature of human overkill? Is the
human-driven loss of megafauna one member of a class, or unique in its tempo
and selectivity?

**Candidate cases:**
- carnivorans entering South America during the interchange;
- dingo arrival in Australia;
- predators reaching islands.

**Why Barry.** His own overkill modelling and the multispecies overkill
simulations (Alroy 2001 [memory]) supply the human-arrival signature that
would be compared.

**Strongest rival.** Interchange extinctions were driven by climate and
habitat change, or by competition rather than predation. The predator's
arrival then marks a time, not a cause.

**Overlaps.** A mutation of Q-037 (whether the present crisis's top-down
signature is unprecedented) and Q-047 (when biotas meet).

### S3. The fossil record of ideas, with this list as its time capsule

**The seed is the list itself.** It is a dated sample of what one active
quantitative palaeobiologist judged most promising in 2003. Twenty-three
years later, each paper's central claim has a fate:
- confirmed;
- refined;
- overturned;
- still open;
- simply forgotten.

**The question.** Do ideas in palaeobiology live and die like taxa? We can ask
the questions the field asks of fossils:
- What is the survival curve of a 2003 hypothesis?
- Is idea death *selective*? Candidate predictors are single against
  multiple lines of evidence, model against data, a glossy-journal debut and
  news coverage.
- Or is it bad luck, as in Raup's question (Q-038), asked of hypotheses?

The list is ideal because it holds whole debates: claim, rebuttal and reply
sit side by side, as with Solé et al. 1997 against Kirchner & Weil 1998.
Those are matched pairs.

**Why it fits EPRP.** It extends the question-first paper's decision-change
ledger from our own decisions to a field's. Claude and Codex could code each
fate independently, with Barry adjudicating disagreements about what his
2003 self meant. That is itself a worked example of transparent human–AI
synthesis.

**Honest flags:**
- This is meta-science, not palaeobiology.
- Coding a "fate" is a judgment and needs a stated rubric before anyone looks
  at outcomes.
- 293 entries include news pieces and book reviews, which would need either
  separate treatment or exclusion.
