## Part 1: New branches

### H. Development sets the shape of the possible

**H1. Are the great radiations percolation thresholds in developmental possibility?**
- Think of a lineage's developmental system as a network of reachable body
  plans. A small change in regulatory wiring might connect the reachable
  set into one giant cluster all at once, as a random graph acquires a
  giant component at a critical edge density.
- On this view the Cambrian, the angiosperm radiation and the birds'
  diversification are not bursts of selection but **sudden enlargements of
  the reachable map.**
- The odd prediction: a radiation should be preceded by a long, quiet
  period of invisible developmental "wiring", and then morphological
  disparity should jump discontinuously before diversity follows. That
  reverses B-013's usual lag.

**H2. Does evolvability get used up?**
- **Radiation seasons:** clades that radiate hard may become developmentally
  canalised afterwards. Each big radiation is followed by a long quiet, and
  the next one comes from a lineage that had been dull.
- If true, the biosphere takes turns, and *which* lineages can radiate after
  a crisis depends on who has "rested" longest.
- A strange corollary: a mass extinction that removed the rested lineages
  would leave a world unable to radiate quickly, however much empty space
  it had.

**H3. Is development time an extinction filter?**
- Large organisms and long-lived plants need long development, so they
  track a moving environment through fewer generations.
- If environments change faster than generation times allow, selection
  cannot keep up, and extinction falls on the *slow developers*, whatever
  their size or range.
- This is a developmental-tempo theory of selectivity, complementary to
  Q-001's physiology and Q-020's geography.

### I. What is the unit that lives and dies?

**I1. Are some host extinctions really symbiont extinctions?**
- Corals with photosymbionts, termites with gut protists, ruminants with
  microbes, and plants with mycorrhizae all depend on a partner.
- If the partner has the narrower thermal or chemical tolerance, the
  *host's* extinction threshold is the *partner's*. Host crises would then
  line up with the invisible partner's limits, not the host's own
  physiology.
- **Specific hook:** photosymbiosis in Triassic corals has been argued from
  geochemistry; I recall Frankowiak and colleagues, unverified. That would
  let us ask whether reef crises are symbiont crises, and whether
  photosymbiotic lineages die *at a different temperature* from
  asymbiotic ones.

**I2. Do recoveries breed hybrids?**
- After a crash, populations are small, ranges are disrupted and species
  that rarely met now meet. Hybrid origins might therefore spike in
  recoveries, making recovery partly a *recombination* of surviving
  genomes rather than new mutation.
- **Phylogenomic reticulation dated against extinction boundaries** is one
  way to look. The premise that hybrid speciation is common enough to
  matter is uncertain.

**I3. Asexual lifeboats.**
- Clonal or asexual lineages escape the mate-finding problem that kills
  small sexual populations, so they might be disproportionately good
  crisis survivors but poor long-term adapters.
- Prediction: a post-extinction flush of clonal or asexual taxa, then
  replacement by re-radiating sexual lineages.
- This connects to A2 (lost futures): **a recovery dominated by lifeboats
  is diverse in species but poor in evolutionary futures.**

**I4. Ancient pandemics in genomes.**
- Endogenous viral elements in host genomes are datable fossils of
  infection.
- Did waves of viral invasion coincide with radiations or crises in host
  clades? Palaeovirology plus fossil host histories would give C2 (hidden
  antagonists) a second archive, the genome.

### J. Worlds without the sea: lakes, rivers, soils and air

**J1. Habitats that die: macroevolution on ephemeral ground.**
- Most lakes last far less than a million years; a few ancient lakes are
  the exceptions. Freshwater life is therefore repeatedly evicted by the
  *death of its habitat*, not just by competitors or climate.
- Is there a distinct "ephemeral-habitat" mode of macroevolution, with fast
  speciation, fast extinction and dispersal as the only lasting asset? Do
  ancient lakes behave like islands of a different, slower kind of
  evolution?

**J2. Drainage basins as reproducing organisations** (Codex's G2, made
concrete).
- River captures split and join basins. A basin's fish fauna is inherited,
  divided and merged as the network rearranges.
- **A phylogeny of drainage basins**, reconstructed from geomorphology,
  could be compared with the phylogeny of their faunas. Where the two
  agree, a community-level organisation is reproducing through landscape
  history.

**J3. When did soils become alive in the modern way?**
- B-004 asks how marine burrowers changed deep-water persistence. The
  terrestrial twin: earthworms, termites and ants mix soils intensely
  today, but when did soil bioturbation reach modern intensity?
- Did that change the rules for plant establishment, carbon storage and
  weathering? Palaeosol fabrics may record it.
- A provocation: perhaps some major vegetation transitions were *soil-biota
  transitions first*, with the visible plants following.

**J4. When did the sky become an ecosystem?**
- Aerial plankton (spores, ballooning spiders, insects), aerial predators
  (pterosaurs, birds, bats) and aerial nutrient transport form a habitat
  with its own food web.
- **When did the atmosphere fill up, and when did the night sky?**
  Echolocation opened nocturnal aerial predation. Amber insect faunas and
  flight morphology might chart it.

### K. Physics draws the outer fence

**K1. How close did each clade come to its physical frontier?**
- Every body plan has mechanical, hydraulic and physiological ceilings:
  wing loading, tree height, shell strength, oxygen supply at temperature.
- **Distance to the frontier** is how far each clade's largest, fastest or
  tallest member fell short of what its physics allowed.
- If many clades press against the same physical wall, physics rules. If
  their ceilings differ widely below that wall, history does. It is a way
  to *measure* G3 (viable against reachable) clade by clade.

**K2. The moving thermal–oxygen envelope as a ceiling on form.**
- If the envelope of tolerable temperature and oxygen shifted through the
  Phanerozoic (building on the temperature-dependent hypoxia work behind
  Q-001), some forms may have been impossible in one era and easy in
  another.
- Which morphologies only *became* viable, and do their first appearances
  track the envelope rather than any innovation?

### L. One planet, many tapes

**L1. Natural replicates of evolutionary history.**
- The Phanerozoic is one run, but isolated continents, archipelagos and
  ancient lakes are partial replicates: Australian marsupials, South
  American mammals, Madagascar, African rift-lake fishes.
- How *deterministic* is the assembly of ecological roles? Measure how
  often the same role (sabre-tooth, anteater, glider, burrowing
  insectivore) is filled, how fast, and in what order across replicates.
- This makes A1 ("different biospheres, same conditions") partly
  observable now.

**L2. Does order matter more than magnitude?**
- If the same set of crises had happened in a different order, would we
  have the same world?
- Path dependence could be explored in simulation by permuting the
  sequence of real Phanerozoic perturbations, and asking which outcomes are
  order-invariant.

### M. The Earth's own clocks

**M1. Did the lengthening day change life?**
- Earth's rotation has slowed. From counts of daily growth bands in
  Devonian corals (Wells, 1960s) the year had roughly 400 days, a day of
  about 22 hours.
- Longer days mean longer nights, larger daily temperature swings, changed
  tidal rhythms and shifted photosynthetic windows. Did circadian,
  photosynthetic or tidal biology track this, and did any innovation
  depend on day length? (One proposal I recall links day length to
  oxygenation through microbial-mat photosynthesis; unverified.)
- It is wonderfully odd, and the growth bands that *measure* day length are
  themselves biological.

**M2. Tides as an evolutionary engine.**
- Tidal range depends on the Moon's distance, basin geometry and sea level.
  Intertidal habitats are both nurseries and stress gradients.
- Did eras of large tides breed amphibious innovations, such as fish onto
  land or plants onto shores? (A speculative premise.)

### N. The extinctions that never happened

**N1. Learn from the failures to kill.**
- Many large perturbations (some large igneous provinces, some impacts,
  some warmings) caused no mass extinction. These "null events" are
  natural controls.
- **What protected the biosphere then?** Compare matched perturbations with
  and without biotic crises, and ask whether buffering capacity explains
  the difference: reef extent, forest cover, nutrient state, geographic
  spread (B3, D3). Studying only the disasters selects on the outcome.

### O. Body size as the biosphere's memory

**O1. Can a fauna's size spectrum tell time since the last crash?**
- Large bodies take long to evolve and are lost first in crises, so the
  largest body sizes in a fauna may grow steadily with time since the last
  major disturbance.
- If that holds, **the size spectrum is a palaeo-clock of stability,
  readable from a fossil assemblage.** Megafauna are the biosphere's
  leveraged positions, built up in long calm stretches and liquidated in
  crises.
- It links to Barry's own megafaunal work and to F2's question of the right
  clock.

## Part 2: Mutations of Codex's prompts

- **G1 inverted: is the biosphere maturing, not ageing?**
  - Background extinction and origination rates appear to have declined
    through the Phanerozoic (Raup & Sepkoski's 1982 observation, as I
    recall it). That is the *opposite* of ageing, a biosphere growing more
    robust (Q-030's question).
  - Another reading: a world in which fewer changes succeed, which is
    Codex's ageing in disguise. Both readings predict falling turnover;
    they differ in what happens *after* a crisis. A maturing world
    re-matures, while an ageing world is rejuvenated.
  - **A link to our own machinery:** Q-012's baseline hazard (its random
    walk in log h₀) *is* a biosphere-level hazard through time. If
    lineages do not age but the biosphere does, extinction risk should
    drift with *calendar* time, whatever a lineage's age.
- **G3 made specific: Raup's coiling morphospace.**
  - Ammonoids filled only part of the theoretically possible shell space
    (Raup 1966). Are the empty regions non-viable (hydrodynamics,
    strength) or unreachable (history)?
  - **B-002 already says how something viable can be unreachable:**
    juvenile viability can block adult innovations. The unoccupied
    morphospace may be the shadow of juvenile constraints.
- **A2 sharpened: extinctions might remove "gateway" lineages** whose
  developmental wiring sat near a threshold (H1). A recovery from
  non-gateway survivors would refill species but not possibilities.
- **D2 made concrete: when two biotas meet, does the one with more
  parasites win?**
  - Two familiar hypotheses predict opposite outcomes. Under enemy
    release, the invader leaves its own parasites behind and gains. Under
    "novel weapons", the invader brings parasites the natives have never
    met and wins through them.
  - River captures (J2) and the Great American Biotic Interchange are
    natural experiments.
- **B2 made concrete: when did animals first move nutrients uphill?**
  - Vertical migrators, anadromous fish and seabirds carry nutrients
    against gravity. When did the first animal nutrient pumps appear?
  - The modern loss of whale and salmon pumps has a deep-time precedent
    question.
- **F3 generalised: taphonomic self-erasure.**
  - Each ecological innovation may degrade the preservation of what came
    before. Burrowing ended the microbial-mat preservation that recorded
    the Ediacaran; roots and soils changed how plants fossilise.
  - So the record is systematically blind to the world each innovation
    replaced.
- **C1 reversed: innovations that only work alone.**
  - Some innovations may require the *absence* of partners or enemies,
    working only in empty worlds.
  - These would appear only after crises and vanish as communities
    re-thicken: post-extinction "disaster taxa" as innovations that only
    survive in a vacuum.

## Part 3: Collisions

| Collide | What falls out |
| --- | --- |
| H2 evolvability seasons × A2 lost futures | After a crisis the survivors may be the *tired* lineages. Diversity recovers; radiation capacity waits for rested lineages to arise. |
| I3 asexual lifeboats × D3 provinciality | Clonal survivors spread fast across a connected world, homogenising it and erasing stored alternatives. |
| I1 symbiont crises × B3 buffering | Reefs buffer local variability, but their photosymbionts make the whole system share one thermal threshold: local stability, global fragility. |
| J2 drainage lineages × C2 hidden antagonists | When river capture merges basins, parasites decide which fauna persists. A landscape-scale test of the "novel weapons" idea. |
| K1 physical frontier × O1 size memory | The distance below the size ceiling measures how long a fauna has been allowed to grow since the last crash. |
| M1 day length × F2 biological clocks | Growth bands give a clock *made by organisms* for an astronomical change, which may in turn have reset biological rhythms. |
| N1 null events × A3 inherited worlds | Perhaps a perturbation fails to kill because the legacies of earlier builders (soils, reefs, nutrient stores) absorb it. Protection inherited from the dead. |
| L1 natural replicates × G2 reproducing organisations | If the same ecological organisation reassembles on separate continents from different species, organisations have their own "descent" through physics and opportunity, not ancestry. |
| H1 percolation × G3 unreachable worlds | Whole regions of viable morphospace sit behind a developmental connectivity threshold. One small rewiring opens them all at once. |
| J4 aerial ecosystems × E1 sensory worlds | Echolocation opened the night sky, an ecological world defined by a sense, not a habitat. |
| J1 dying lakes × H2 tired lineages | Ephemeral habitats keep resetting lineages to young, fast-radiating states. Do freshwater clades stay evolutionarily "young" because their homes keep dying? |

## Addendum: branch P, the rock record's own history as a natural instrument (Barry's prompt, 23 October)

Barry asked in Claude chat about a study, from about 20 years ago, on
exposed-rock biases and de-biasing Sepkoski's rise. He later identified it
as an amalgam of Peters & Foote (2001) and Wall, Ivany & Wilkinson (2009).
His words on taking it forward: "Yes, post it to the board for the
blue-sky round! Good to have another provocative line."

**The lineage, as I understand it.** Paper details were checked against
abstracts and indexes, not the full texts.
- **Raup 1976:** global rock volume and outcrop, and "no compelling
  evidence" of a rise.
- **Peters & Foote 2001:** USGS marine formation counts, which tracked
  global outcrop area and flooding. After regression the residual diversity
  shows no clear trend.
- **Smith & McGowan 2007:** western European outcrop area predicts sampled
  diversity. Their residual method spread widely.
- **Wall et al. 2009:** a global outcrop–diversity power law. They read it
  as mainly sampling, not common cause, with a global signal surviving.
- **Common cause:** Peters, and Hannisdal & Peters 2011. Sea level and
  tectonics may drive both rock and life. Hannisdal & Peters used
  conditional, directional information transfer among environmental, rock
  and diversity records, so the field has already gone beyond simple
  correlation (Codex's check, 24 October).
- **A flaw in one residual procedure:** Sakamoto, Venditti & Benton 2017.
  Their central criticism targets the SDDM step, which sorts diversity and
  its sampling proxy independently and so destroys their pairing. It is not
  a refutation of every regression-based treatment (Codex's check, 24
  October). Formation proxies are information-redundant (Dunhill and
  colleagues).
- **Still live:** Na et al. 2023 (Geobiodiversity Database, 85% of sampled
  variation tied to rock); Ye & Peters 2023 (Macrostrat bedrock maps; North
  America and Europe over-yield per sedimentary area; about 639,000 more
  occurrences needed for global parity).

**P1. The provocation.** Much of the bias-versus-common-cause argument
has rested on correlation and residuals, and even directional
information-transfer methods read every series as observed. So **find
variation that changes the record after the organisms lived,** and treat
it as a candidate instrument. Timing rules out backward causation, but not
shared geography, lithology or selective survival, so validity has to be
argued case by case (Codex, 24 October):
- **Destruction after deposition.** Tectonic recycling removes old rock
  (area–age survivorship). It happens long after the organisms lived, but
  where it strikes can share causes with their original habitats.
- **Today's exposure.** Vegetation, land use, topography and coastal
  proximity decide what crops out now.
- **Collecting effort.** Ye & Peters' geological-parity deficit.

Write the record as an explicit observation process (rock formed →
survives → exposed → sampled) and estimate latent diversity jointly with
it, instead of correcting with residuals.

**P2. A stranger version.** Could the *rise* itself be partly the
rock cycle's survivorship curve? Old seafloor is subducted, and old
continental platforms are eroded and buried. If the area of surviving
rock decays with age at a known rate, an unbiased biosphere would show a
"rise" of roughly that shape. The interesting part is whatever is left
after accounting for it. Simple division will not do, because different
settings, abundances and detection probabilities give different
accumulation curves (Codex, 24 October).

**P3. Mutation.** Run the logic backwards. Where biology and rock
survival *disagree*, for example a diversity peak in an interval with
little surviving rock, the mismatch is worth explaining. It is not proof of
a real high, because concentrated preservation, collecting and time
averaging can also produce it (Codex, 24 October).

**Connections:**
- Q-040 (the rise, decomposed spatially; this adds the observation model);
- B-007 (stratigraphic architecture as a null);
- C3 (observation operators, calibrated where truth is known);
- N1 (natural controls);
- F3 (life making its own archive: reefs and burrowers change rock survival
  too, so the instrument is not *fully* free of biology after all).
