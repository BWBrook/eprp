# Extinction and recovery: what is actually in dispute?

Codex, 28 October 2026. A focused, opportunistic synthesis for EPRP, not a
systematic review or a proposal to change the question register. The aim is to
identify biological disagreements that can be confronted with evidence, not to
rank all extinction questions. The strongest opportunities are (1) whether
reorganisation predicts subsequent ecological response beyond richness and
external forcing, and (2) whether biological losses helped maintain the
physical conditions that inhibited recovery. A third, descriptive comparison
asks which aspects of ecosystem structure returned and which changed.

## The central problem: a recovered number is not a recovered ecosystem

A single recovery duration conflates at least four targets: taxonomic richness;
the presence and relative representation of ecological roles; community
composition and interactions; and a process such as carbon export. These can
return at different times, or establish a new state. Functional categories
inferred from traits are not direct measurements of energy flow, feeding links,
or the resilience of the whole ecosystem. A local refuge can demonstrate that
a type of community existed at a time; it cannot establish its global extent.
A post-event match to the pre-event value is also not proof that the event
caused an observed change or that the old state was the only possible baseline.

### 1. Early complex ecosystems versus slow bottom-up rebuilding

[Chen and Benton (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1038/ngeo1475), full review and
Fig. 4, describe an early episode of diversification followed by setbacks and
propose progressive rebuilding from lower to higher trophic levels. The full
paper is more nuanced than a slogan of no recovery for 8–9 Myr: it recognises
rapid rebound in some groups and says high-level consumers may appear early.
Its later endpoint is a stable, complex, longer-term structure. The
[Guiyang biota](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adf1622) (Dai et al. 2023,
abstract), dated by the authors to 1.08 ± 0.08 Myr after the extinction,
contains diverse fish and crustaceans and is interpreted as a trophically complex
marine community. This is evidence against a universal absence of complex
communities that late; its exceptional preservation and single locality do not
by themselves establish widespread or stable recovery. [Friesenbichler et al.
(2021)](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.11654), full paper, examine 37
lithological units and 12 guilds in a marine level-bottom synthesis and report
major Middle Triassic diversification and ecological reorganisation.

The apparent opposition partly dissolves when local appearance, global group
richness and restoration of a community structure are distinguished. A live
question remains: **were early complex communities isolated or frequent across
comparable habitats, and did they persist through subsequent environmental
perturbations?** [Nicholls et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44185-025-00117-2)
is a useful full-text perspective; its reconciliation is an interpretation,
not an independent test of a common assembly rule.

**Test:** start with a bounded synthesis of published, independently dated
Early Triassic marine assemblages, not an immediate PBDB-wide campaign. For
each locality and interval record depositional setting, preservation mode,
sampling effort, dating uncertainty, observable trophic categories, and whether
each category is detected. Compare like habitats and preservation modes across
time and, where possible, adjacent regions. Treat exceptional deposits as a
separate observation process; do not code failure to find a group as its true
absence. Compare the timing and persistence of *observed multi-role
assemblages* against the later rise of richness and relative abundance, with
uncertainty intervals and alternative age correlations. An independent second
fossil horizon at comparable habitat would challenge an isolated-refuge
account; a single recurring Lagerstätte cannot establish global prevalence.
The practical outcome is a documented spatial and temporal contrast, not proof
of bottom-up or top-down causation. This is a narrower route than B-001's
cross-event guild-order contract, currently on hold. Its previous PBDB support
counts are themselves under a data-access hold and must not be used as evidence.

### 2. Does ecological organisation matter after richness has returned?

[Alvarez et al. (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-019-1590-8), full
accepted manuscript, analyse a densely sampled 13-Myr nannoplankton series at
ODP Site 1209. They report approximately 1.8 Myr of unusually variable
community composition after K–Pg, followed by a more stable assemblage, while
richness was still low. Their stability metric is variation in relative
abundances; the carbon-cycle and export claims use additional proxies. This
supports decoupling of several measured dimensions at one site, not a direct
measurement of the resistance of an entire ocean ecosystem to a controlled
perturbation.

An especially relevant new comparison is [Jones et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1029/2026PA005729),
published on 24 October. The authors report that high-dominance nannoplankton
successions persisted at El Kef for at least 3.7 Myr, longer than the
approximately 1.8-Myr unstable interval at Site 1209. They attribute much of
the difference to palaeoenvironmental setting, while noting unresolved regional
and chronology contrasts. These are different sites, sample histories and
metrics; the difference does not show that an identical perturbation was
counterfactually experienced for longer at one site. Their inference about
biological-pump recovery is conditional on proxy assumptions, and they note
that paired planktic–benthic isotope measurements are unavailable for El Kef.
The publication's abstract and selected accessible sections were checked; its
full data analysis has not been independently reproduced.

[Edie, Collins and Jablonski (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adv1171),
full article, compare marine bivalve genera in the Maastrichtian, the survivor
pool and the present. They report 266/438 genera lost and 2/37 categorical
functional groups lost, with marked later reshuffling within groups. The four
categorical traits approximate ecological roles; the comparison does not
observe ecological function or reconstruct the complete recovery trajectory.
[Dunhill et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-53000-2), full
article, reconstruct feasible trait-based food webs from one Toarcian basin;
several structural metrics precede richness in returning. Their links and
secondary extinctions are modelled, not observed feeding relationships. The
studies support dissociation, but do not settle whether a changed composition
makes a later disturbance better or worse.

**Live disagreement:** does functional composition or the identity of
incumbents carry information about a later ecological response *beyond*
richness, environment and observation history? The strong rival is that the
apparent memory is caused by persistent environmental regimes, survivor-pool
composition, shared clade history, sampling, or omitted state. This is the
existing Q-009 question; mere demonstration of decoupled trajectories is
already occupied.

**Test:** inspect whether the Alvarez deposit contains dated, consistently
sampled species abundances and independent forcing and outcome series for
multiple later perturbations. Before fitting, define an outcome independent of
the predictors (for example a later, separately measured recovery of community
composition or process proxy), with the same age model and explicit sampling
uncertainty. Compare a richness-plus-environment baseline against the same
baseline augmented by prespecified composition summaries, using forward
holdout by disturbance/time block, not random samples from adjacent time
points. Match predictor information windows and quantify missingness,
temporal dependence and the number of independent perturbations. Retain a
negative or imprecise result: a short or sparse series may support only a
case study, not a general test. Added prediction is not by itself mediation or
proof that composition causes resilience. Do not create a new analysis until
the source data, independent outcomes and viable holdouts have been checked.

**Concrete data lead:** Alvarez's [PANGAEA series](https://doi.org/10.1594/PANGAEA.893901)
includes relative abundance, clade and size summaries. Its [Table A](https://doi.org/10.1594/PANGAEA.893899)
is interpolated onto 13-kyr intervals and contains selected abundant taxa,
so its rows are neither independent raw samples nor a complete composition.
[Table E](https://doi.org/10.1594/PANGAEA.893919) supplies sample/depth/age
information, but access to the raw counts themselves remains to be checked.
Jones's separate [El Kef dataset](https://doi.org/10.1594/PANGAEA.997795)
reports 186 samples and contains [raw taxon counts](https://doi.org/10.1594/PANGAEA.997794).
These are an unusually timely cross-setting comparison, but harmonised age,
taxon, observation and environmental covariates are not yet established.
The sites differ in setting as well as their histories, so two-site contrasts
cannot identify which factor caused the difference.

A first direct inspection of the deposited TSVs found 997 interpolated rows
in Alvarez Table A and 990 unique sample labels in Table E; the latter is
sample/age metadata, not the full species count matrix. The Jones all-taxa
file has 199 unique hole–core–depth keys and no age column; its dominant-genera
file has 186 keys, all present in the raw file, and two populated age-model
columns. The reason 13 raw keys are absent from the dominant table has not been
established. These are file-level counts, not independent fossil observations
or a completed join. PANGAEA labels the inspected Alvarez Table A
CC BY-NC-ND 3.0 and the Jones all-taxa file CC BY 4.0; preserve the original
files and assess terms separately before sharing derived products.

### 3. Did biological damage prolong the environmental crisis?

[Hull (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2015.08.053), full review,
proposes Earth-system succession, in which changing post-extinction biota and
biogeochemical reservoirs interact over extended intervals. This is a
mechanistic framing, not evidence that a particular feedback was historically
dominant. [Isson et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-31128-3),
full article, combine a carbon–silica model, a 36-locality chert compilation
and mineralogical evidence from two Japanese sections. They propose that the
loss of siliceous organisms increased silica availability and reverse
weathering, retaining CO₂ and prolonging warmth. Their model shows a possible
mechanism under its assumptions; chert occurrence and clay assemblages are
proxies influenced by preservation and depositional conditions, and agreement
with a model does not identify a unique historical cause.

**Live disagreement:** biological feedback versus sustained or repeated
external forcing, other weathering responses, or both. In particular, the same
warming and anoxia can drive both loss of silicifiers and changes in
sedimentary silica, producing correlated signals without the proposed
feedback being the dominant cause.

**Test:** use the existing Q-021 work and its source/observation contracts,
not a new Earth-system model. Build a common time and proxy table for the
silica, clay, carbon-cycle, temperature and redox records, preserving section,
facies, age-model and measurement provenance. Compare published simulations
or a small matched model ensemble in which forcing histories and observations
are shared, with biological silica feedback active versus suppressed; allow
external forcing and alternative reverse-weathering pathways in both. Seek
predictions that differ in timing and magnitude for independently measured
silica burial, authigenic minerals and temperature, and explicitly test
sensitivity to age alignment, preservation and model parameters. First inspect
whether the published model and input trajectories actually make that matched
comparison possible. A good fit to a single carbon-isotope series is insufficient
to assign a feedback, and a negative comparison would not exclude other
biological contributions.

## Recommendation and limits

The best first substantive move is the Q-009 data-and-outcome feasibility
check, paired with the bounded local-assemblage synthesis above. It addresses a
biological consequence of ecological reorganisation rather than only the
order in which indicators change; a well-resolved time series gives a possible
first test. It is not yet known whether independent subsequent outcomes and
enough perturbations exist, and the existing publication may already occupy
some candidate contrasts. The environmental-feedback comparison is exciting
but has higher proxy and causal-identification demands; keep it linked to the
existing Q-021 work. These are recommendations for discussion, not adopted
priorities, novelty claims, or an authorisation to start a primary project.
